Unanswered problems inspired the next book.

The 1641 edition of Jinkoki closed with twelve problems and withheld the answers. Later authors responded and added challenges of their own. A printed book became one turn in a longer mathematical conversation.

Mathematics was displayed at temples and shrines.

A sangaku could commemorate a solution, honor a teacher, challenge visitors, or display mathematical skill. Surviving tablets cannot speak for every local motive, but they show that geometry occupied public cultural space.

324 problems recorded in a travel diary.

Yamaguchi Kanzan’s mathematical travel diary records hundreds of problems encountered on journeys. It reveals knowledge moving through face-to-face meetings and local networks, not only through famous schools in Edo.

To calculate faster, study the error.

Takebe’s numerical work did more than extend a list of digits. It examined how successive approximations changed. Modern reconstructions compare this with convergence acceleration, but must state exactly which historical quantities are being transformed.

After six spheres, the chain returns.

An 1822 sangaku associated with Irisawa Shintaro Hiroatsu gives a six-sphere configuration equivalent to the chain later discussed by Soddy. The historical achievement is remarkable; the modern theorem used to render it should not be mistaken for the original notation.

Can remainders identify a number?

If a number leaves known remainders on division by several moduli, those fragments may determine it up to a repeating period. The classic 3–5–7 example travels from Chinese mathematics into Japanese computation.

Wasan did not disappear with educational reform.

Meiji school policy favored Western mathematics, but people, manuscripts, local practice, collecting, and historical scholarship continued. “Replacement” describes an institutional shift; it should not be confused with instantaneous cultural disappearance.
